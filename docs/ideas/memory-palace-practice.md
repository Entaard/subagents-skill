# Building a Memory Palace: A Practice Guide

This guide is structured as six levels, easiest to hardest, each with a concrete exercise you can perform today and a self-test for when to move on. Work the levels in order; do not skip to abstract material or number systems before your first real palace is stable — the beginner mistakes section at each level explains why. For the theory behind each step — why the classical rules exist, what the evidence says about how well the technique works, and where its limits are — see the companion document, `memory-palace-anatomy.md`.

## Level 0 — Test drive

**Goal.** Prove to yourself, in one sitting, that the mechanism works at all, before investing in a real palace.

**What to do.** Use your own home as the space. Walk a short, obvious route through it in your head — front door, shoe rack, bathroom, kitchen, living room — and mentally place one image per stop. This is the standard entry drill across practitioner sources: Art of Memory's beginner guide and Nelson Dellis's *Remember It!* "See-Link-Go!" workflow both teach it as the first step, with a short grocery list mapped one-per-locus onto household stops (Art of Memory blog; Dellis via summary sources — search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**One concrete exercise.**
1. Write down a 10-item list (any list — a shopping list works well).
2. Pick ten loci — one per item — along a route you already know without thinking: front door, shoe rack, hallway mirror, bathroom sink, kitchen counter, stove, fridge, kitchen table, living room couch, TV.
3. For each item, form one vivid image of it interacting with that locus — not just sitting there, but doing something to it. If item 1 is "milk," picture milk spilling off the shoe rack, not a milk carton parked next to it.
4. Walk the route once in your head, placing each image as you go.
5. Wait a few minutes, doing something else.
6. Walk the route again, in order, and say out loud what you see at each locus.

**Ready to progress when** you can recall all 10 items in order without looking at the list, in one attempt, right after doing the exercise once.

**Common mistakes.** Placing objects passively next to a locus instead of making them interact with it; building an elaborate connected story across loci instead of one direct, self-contained scene per stop (thecraftofmemory.substack.com, search snippet, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**Time expectation.** No sourced figure for this specific drill; expect it to take under fifteen minutes end to end, image-building included.

## Level 1 — First real palace

**Goal.** Build a stable, reusable palace of meaningful size, and confirm the route itself is reliable before you ever load content into it.

**What to do.** Choose one familiar place — again, usually home — and lay out a fixed, non-crossing route of about 20 loci through it. Before placing anything, drill the empty route: recite it forward and backward from memory several times. Art of Memory's guidance is explicit that this route-familiarization step should happen *before* loading content, recommending at least five clean run-throughs (Art of Memory blog, search snippet, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**One concrete exercise.**
1. Walk your chosen space and write down 20 distinct, ordered loci — for example, one object or spot per room, moving in one consistent direction (never doubling back or crossing your own path).
2. Recite the 20 loci from memory, forward, at least five times, until you don't hesitate or skip one.
3. Recite the same 20 loci backward, at least five times, until that's clean too.
4. Only once both directions are clean, take a 20-item list (a longer shopping list, or 20 vocabulary words) and place one image per locus, following the encoding craft from Level 0: large, active, exaggerated, interacting with the locus.
5. Walk the loaded route forward and recall all 20 items in order.

**Ready to progress when** you get a full 20-out-of-20 recall, in order, after a single loading pass — and you can also recite the empty route backward without hesitation (Art of Memory, search snippet, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**Common mistakes.** Skipping the empty-route drill and loading content onto a route you haven't actually tested — this is one of the most consistently named beginner errors across sources; choosing a route that is visually complex or self-crossing, which undermines the ordering the whole technique depends on (Art of Memory / craft-of-memory guidance, search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**Time expectation.** No sourced figure for how long route-drilling itself takes; expect the empty-route repetitions to take a few minutes, with the full loaded exercise taking longer than Level 0 in proportion to the extra 10 items.

## Level 2 — Palace fleet + real use

**Goal.** Move from a single practice palace to multiple palaces used for real material, and add a maintenance schedule so what you encode actually survives.

**What to do.** Once your first palace is full and reliable, build a second and, eventually, a third palace of similar size — sources describe this qualitatively as "start small, gradually expand," without giving a fixed numeric trigger for when to add a new palace (search snippets, various beginner guides, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26). Start using palaces for real content, not just practice lists: a common application is memorizing the structure of a speech or a set of talking points, one point per locus. At this level, also adopt a maintenance schedule — without one, encoded material fades. The most concretely documented schedule is Dominic O'Brien's "Rule of Five": review immediately after encoding, again 24 hours later, then at one week, one month, and three months (Art of Memory forum / treasurers.org, search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**One concrete exercise.**
1. Take an upcoming talk, presentation, or set of talking points you actually need — 15 to 20 discrete points.
2. Build or reuse a second palace (a different familiar route from Level 1's) and place one talking point per locus, using the same active-image craft.
3. Rehearse the talk once using only the walk as your outline.
4. Schedule four follow-up reviews on the Rule of Five cadence: same day, next day, one week out, one month out.

**Ready to progress when** you can deliver the talk from the palace alone (no notes) after the first two Rule-of-Five checkpoints (same-day and next-day review), and you're comfortable holding two palaces without confusing which route holds which content.

**Common mistakes.** Jumping to multiple palaces or abstract material before the first palace is fully mastered — several sources converge on mastering one small palace completely before expanding (Art of Memory / Mullen Memory, search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26). On reuse specifically: whether to reuse a palace for new material later is a genuine, unresolved disagreement among practitioners — Alex Mullen reports not actively clearing palaces between uses, while others on the Art of Memory forum recommend giving each reuse a distinct theme, or simply not reusing the same route on the same day, to avoid interference (mullenmemory.com; forum.artofmemory.com threads; search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26). There is no settled answer here — pick a policy, apply it consistently, and watch for confusion as your own signal.

**Time expectation.** The only concrete figure available across sources is Mullen's recommendation of roughly one month of ramp-up to become palace-proficient before a school term starts (mullenmemory.com, search snippet, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26); treat that as an order-of-magnitude guide for reaching comfortable, real-use proficiency, not a guarantee.

## Level 3 — Abstract material + study integration

**Goal.** Extend the technique from concrete objects to abstract material (definitions, facts, vocabulary), and pair palace encoding with spaced-repetition software for long-term retention.

**What to do.** Abstract concepts don't have an obvious image, so they need a conversion step: use sound-alikes or puns (for example, "hero" encoded via an image of Hercules) or a standard symbol with high imageability (a dove for peace) (search snippets, magneticmemorymethod.com / artofmemory.com, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26). Once you're encoding real study material this way, pair it with spaced repetition rather than relying on palace review alone. Alex Mullen's documented approach stores course notes in Anki with a dedicated "Mnemonic" field describing the palace image for each fact, organized into hierarchical decks by subject; he treats memory techniques, spaced repetition, and practice questions as a three-part system, stating plainly that he can't imagine learning without spaced repetition even with the aid of memory palaces (mullenmemory.com, fetched/searched 2026-08-26). In other words: the palace does the initial encoding and short-term ordered recall; the SRS does the long-term durability layer.

**One concrete exercise.**
1. Pick 15 abstract terms from something you're actually studying (definitions, vocabulary, or named concepts).
2. For each term, invent a sound-alike or symbol image, then place it in a fresh palace or the next open stretch of loci in an existing one.
3. Create one Anki (or equivalent spaced-repetition) card per term, and add a note field describing the palace image you used for it — this is the field Mullen calls "Mnemonic."
4. Run the deck on your SRS's default schedule for two weeks.

**Ready to progress when** you can walk the palace and recall all 15 terms correctly without looking at your notes, and your SRS review accuracy on those same 15 cards is holding steady (not dropping) after two weeks of scheduled review.

**Common mistakes.** Relying on the palace alone for durability instead of adding scheduled review — Mullen's own framing is that palaces without spaced repetition are not enough for him to trust long-term learning (mullenmemory.com, fetched 2026-08-26); trying to encode abstract material before the concrete-object mechanics from Levels 0–2 are solid.

**Time expectation.** No sourced figure for how long abstract-material encoding takes relative to concrete items; expect it to take noticeably longer per item than Level 1's concrete-object encoding, since each term needs an extra conversion step before it can be imaged.

## Level 4 — Number and card systems

**Goal.** Add dedicated encoding systems for digits and playing cards, layered on top of the loci mechanics you already have — not a new technique, but a new alphabet of images to place into your existing palaces.

**What to do.** Learn the Major System first: a fixed mapping from digits to consonant sounds, which lets you convert any number into a word or phrase you can image. Once the Major System is fluent, move to PAO (Person-Action-Object), which typically encodes two-digit groups as a single person performing a single action on a single object, or the Dominic System, which maps digit pairs to people's initials — a system developed and competition-tested by eight-time world memory champion Dominic O'Brien. Each of these produces images that go into loci exactly as in earlier levels; the Art of Memory wiki treats the Journey Method, Major System, PAO, and Dominic System as successive layers built on the same loci scaffold, not as separate techniques (search snippets, artofmemory.com wiki, Wikipedia "Dominic system," fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**What these buy you.** Concrete or abstract-but-nameable content (Levels 0–3) can already be imaged directly. Numbers and card sequences can't — a digit has no inherent image — so these systems exist specifically to give arbitrary numeric or card data an image to attach to a locus. This is the layer that makes competitive digit and card memorization possible.

**One concrete exercise.**
1. Learn the standard Major System mapping for digits 0–9 (a fixed table of digit-to-consonant-sound correspondences; use any standard published table, since none is detailed in the sourced notes for this guide).
2. Convert a 20-digit number into 10 two-digit-group images using the Major System.
3. Place the 10 images into 10 loci in an existing palace.
4. Walk the palace and read the digits back off, in order.

**Ready to progress when** you can convert a fresh 20-digit number to images and recall it correctly with one pass through the palace, without hesitating over the digit-to-sound mapping itself.

**Common mistakes.** None specific to this level are named in the sourced practitioner material beyond the general encoding-craft mistakes already covered (over-elaborate narratives, passive rather than interactive images); apply those same fixes here.

**Time expectation.** No sourced figure for how long it takes to become fluent in the Major System or PAO. The only numeric benchmarks available in the sources are elite competition results, not beginner timelines: the classical-competition speed-card record is 12.25 seconds (one source shows an earlier reported 12.74 seconds), separate from a non-classical televised challenge time of 17.593 seconds (forum.artofmemory.com / worldmemorychampionships.com, search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26) — useful for calibration, not as a target.

## Level 5 — Mastery

**Goal.** Operate the technique at speed, scale journeys across many locations, and treat your palace inventory as a standing, lifelong knowledge store rather than a disposable study aid.

**What to do.** At this level, practice shifts toward speed (timed recall of cards and numbers), scale (journeys spanning multiple buildings or a neighborhood for lists of 50–100+ items), and inventory management. Mullen's documented habit points toward treating memory palaces as a long-term knowledge infrastructure rather than a single-use trick: he keeps a running spreadsheet inventory of his memory palaces and their current usage (mullenmemory.com, search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26). Advanced practitioners also train on shorter-duration disciplines (a minute or less) alongside the longer classical five-minute-plus competition formats, for rapid-fire practice (search snippet, arXiv memory-competitions survey note, 2025 — treat as description, not endorsement, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**What competition level looks like, as calibration, not requirement.** Elite benchmarks exist for calibration only — most practitioners using this technique for study or professional recall have no reason to approach them. Journalist Joshua Foer's account of going from novice to winning the US memory championship in roughly one year (competing 2005, winning 2006) is the only general beginner-to-expert timeline located in the sources, and it is a single, qualitative anecdote, not a typical outcome (Foer, *Moonwalking with Einstein*, via Shortform/Derek Sivers summaries, search snippets, fetched-via-search 2026-08-26).

**One concrete exercise.**
1. Build or extend a journey spanning at least two separate locations (for example, your home plus your commute route) totaling 50 or more loci.
2. Load it with a real 50-item list — a substantial study set, a long agenda, or a full set of terms from a course unit.
3. Time yourself walking the full journey for recall, and log the time and accuracy.
4. Add the journey to a running written inventory of your palaces (name, size, current content, last-reviewed date), following Mullen's spreadsheet approach.
5. Repeat with a fresh 50-item list one week later, comparing your time and accuracy against the first attempt.

**Ready to progress when** — there is no further level; treat "ready" here as: your second attempt is faster and at least as accurate as your first, and your palace inventory is current enough that you could tell someone else, without checking, which palaces are in use and what's in them.

**Common mistakes.** None specific to this level are named in the sourced material; the general mistakes from earlier levels (passive placement, over-elaborate scenes, unreviewed route stability) still apply at scale and are worth re-checking whenever a large journey starts producing errors.

**Time expectation.** No sourced figure for time-to-mastery beyond Foer's one-year anecdote above; expect continued, open-ended improvement in speed and scale rather than a fixed endpoint — competitive practitioners keep training for years.
